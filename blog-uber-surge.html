Predicting Uber Surge Pricing on 36M NYC Trips

A surge-pricing classifier built end to end - engineering the target, guarding against leakage, tuning XGBoost with out-of-time validation, and an honest look at why the ceiling is data, not the model.

XGBoost Optuna SHAP Python scikit-learn NYC TLC Data

Why I Built This

I wanted a project that mirrored a real production ML problem - imbalanced classes, a target that doesn't exist off the shelf, temporal leakage traps, and a genuine performance ceiling. Predicting surge pricing from NYC ride data hits all of those. The goal: given the hour, zone, and weather, is this trip likely to cost more than usual?

Data: ~36M NYC TLC high-volume for-hire trips (Uber/Lyft), January-February 2023, joined to hourly Central Park weather. Cleaned to ~35M rows.

The Hardest Part: Defining "Surge"

The dataset has no surge label. My first instinct - flag trips whose fare-per-mile exceeds 1.5× the hourly median - turned out to be badly confounded. Fare-per-mile is inflated for short trips (the fixed base fare spread over few miles) and slow trips (the per-minute charge), so that label would mostly capture trip geometry, not pricing pressure.

The fix: compare each trip's fare to what it should cost given its distance and time. I fit a robust (Huber) model of expected fare, then flagged trips priced well above it.

# expected fare from distance + time (robust to outliers)
model = HuberRegressor().fit(fit[['trip_miles', 'trip_time_mins']],
                            fit['base_passenger_fare'])
df['expected_fare'] = model.predict(df[['trip_miles', 'trip_time_mins']])

# surge = charged >50% above the normal price for a trip that size
df['surge_mult'] = df['base_passenger_fare'] / df['expected_fare']
df['is_surge']   = (df['surge_mult'] > 1.5).astype(int)

A robust regression matters here: ordinary least squares would let the surge trips themselves pull the fitted line upward, hiding the very thing I was trying to detect. The resulting positive rate was ~6% - a moderately imbalanced problem. I also dropped trips under 0.5 miles, where fare-per-mile explodes; the 0.5 cutoff came from a "knee" plot rather than a guess.

Features

Everything a rider could know before the trip - and nothing derived from the fare (that would be leakage).

Location
Pickup zone (smoothed target encoding) and borough (one-hot). Zone turned out to be the dominant signal.
Time
Hour and day-of-week (raw + cyclical sin/cos), weekend and holiday flags.
Demand
Trip counts per zone lagged 1h / 24h / 168h plus 3h / 24h rolling averages, shifted to avoid leaking the current hour.
Weather
Temperature, precipitation, and a rain flag - which the EDA already hinted would be weak.

Two details did most of the leakage-proofing. Zone is high-cardinality (~260 values), so I target-encoded it - replacing each zone with its smoothed historical surge rate, fit on training data only:

# smoothed surge rate per zone, learned from TRAIN only
stats = y_train.groupby(X_train['PULocationID']).agg(['mean', 'count'])
te = (stats['mean']*stats['count'] + global_mean*20) / (stats['count'] + 20)
X_test['zone_te'] = X_test['PULocationID'].map(te).fillna(global_mean)

The smoothing (the +20) pulls zones with little data toward the global rate, so a zone with three trips doesn't get an extreme, unreliable value - the same shrinkage idea used for rare merchants in fraud models.

Method

Clean + label → Features → Time-based split
↓
Logistic baseline → XGBoost (Optuna) → SHAP

The split is time-based (train on earlier weeks, test on the last two), never random - a random split would let the model peek at the future through the lag features. I started with a logistic regression baseline, then an XGBoost tuned with Optuna (30 trials, PR-AUC objective, early stopping). Imbalance was handled with scale_pos_weight, and I evaluated with PR-AUC, not accuracy - at 6% positives, a "never surge" model scores 94% accuracy and catches nothing.

A validation trap I caught

My first tuning run reported a rosy PR-AUC of 0.37, but the test set came back at 0.19. The cause: the tuning validation slice was drawn randomly from the same weeks as training, so it measured in-distribution fit, not future prediction. Switching to an out-of-time validation slice brought the tuning score down to a truthful ~0.21 that matched the test set. In-distribution validation lies when you're predicting the future.

PR-AUC is judged against the base rate (5.2%), not 0.5 - so 0.188 is ~3.6× better than chance. The threshold is a business dial, not a fixed 0.5: lower it to catch more surges (recall up to ~88% at low precision), raise it for trustworthy alerts (precision ~20-40% at lower recall). You pick the point from the cost of a false alarm versus a miss.

Notably, logistic already reached 3.0× - so much of the signal is additive and monotonic (mostly the zone effect). XGBoost's extra lift came from non-linear interactions like hour×zone (rush hour matters differently by location), but that gain was modest.

What Drives Surge (SHAP)

SHAP told a clean, intuitive story:

  • Zone dominates - roughly 7× any other feature. Where a trip starts is the biggest predictor.
  • Time - rush hours and weekends push toward surge.
  • Recent demand - high trip volume in the last few hours raises surge odds.
  • Weather is weak - temperature has a small effect, rain barely registers, confirming the EDA.

Crucially, no fare-derived feature appears - the leakage guards held.

The Honest Ceiling

The most important finding is why performance plateaus at ~3.6×. Surge is fundamentally a demand-versus-supply gap - prices spike when riders outstrip available drivers. It can fire from either side: a demand surge, or drivers simply going offline. My data records completed trips, so it captures demand and context but has no supply signal (idle drivers, unfilled requests, wait times). Half the equation is missing.

That's the real ceiling - not hyperparameters. It's why a linear model nearly keeps up, why tuning didn't move the test score, and why the highest-ROI next step would be adding driver-availability data. Naming that honestly matters more to me than inflating a number.

What I Learned

The modeling was the easy part; the judgment calls were the project. Constructing a defensible target, catching in-distribution validation optimism, choosing PR-AUC over accuracy, reading the threshold as a business decision, and diagnosing the ceiling as a data limitation rather than a model one - those are the transferable skills. The same playbook (severe imbalance, interaction-heavy signal, leakage discipline, cost-based thresholds) is exactly what a fraud or churn model needs.

Next steps I'm considering: supply-side features if I can source them, a richer target (per-zone fare models, snowfall in the weather join), and a GCP dashboard to serve zone-level surge risk.
